Component 1

Company Overview

Piva Capital is a San Francisco-based venture capital firm founded in 2019 by Ricardo Angel. It invests in breakthrough technologies intended to usher in a new industrial era, with a focus on sectors that many traditional SaaS-oriented VCs find difficult: energy, heavy industry, materials, production, mobility, robotics, industrial software, grid infrastructure, operational resilience, and decarbonization.

Piva’s first fund was a $250 million vehicle backed by PETRONAS. PETRONAS describes Piva as its venture arm based in San Francisco, with a mandate to invest in companies at the inflection of scientific breakthrough and mass-market impact in North America and Europe. Piva operates with strategic industrial connectivity but positions itself as an independent investment firm backing early-to-growth-stage companies around the world.

Because Piva is a private venture capital firm, it does not disclose revenue by product line. Students should understand its business through the VC model: assets under management, management fees, carried interest, portfolio construction, technical diligence, follow-on financing, board work, corporate partnerships, and exits. For this visit, the key question is how deep-tech venture capital differs from software venture capital.

Component 2

Strategic Context — The “What”

Piva Capital focuses on the intersection of deep tech, AI, energy, industrial systems, and the physical world. Its investment thesis looks beyond SaaS and asks which hardware, software, materials, energy, and automation technologies will reshape the real economy. This makes Piva a useful contrast to the other Silicon Valley visits: it is less about chatbots and productivity tools, and more about AI meeting factories, grids, supply chains, utilities, metals, robotics, and critical infrastructure.

Piva’s 2025 responsible investment report argues that AI is accelerating software cycles, but physical bottlenecks in hardware, infrastructure, and energy remain. Its core message is that software constraints are giving way to hardware realities: AI’s appetite for power is pushing against today’s energy system, supply chains are being redrawn by geopolitics and resource competition, and climate-tech value propositions increasingly need to win on economics rather than aspiration.

Several portfolio examples illustrate this thesis. Urbint uses AI-powered operational resilience tools to help utilities predict and prevent incidents affecting workers and critical infrastructure; Itron signed a definitive agreement in 2025 to acquire Urbint for $325 million. Boston Metal is pursuing molten oxide electrolysis for lower-carbon metals and green steel. Xage Security focuses on zero-trust security for critical infrastructure and announced an NVIDIA BlueField integration for AI factories and critical infrastructure. VEIR, Planted Solar and other infrastructure-oriented companies show how AI growth creates energy, grid and deployment bottlenecks.

The Asia angle is important. Piva’s PETRONAS connection makes it structurally relevant to Asian industrial capital and customers, even though Piva’s PETRONAS mandate is described as North America and Europe. PETRONAS also has Twin Towers Ventures covering APAC and MENA. This gives students a concrete case of how Silicon Valley deep-tech innovation connects to Asian energy, industrial and strategic capital.

Executive interpretation: Piva’s strategic message is that the AI economy will not scale on software alone. The next bottlenecks are energy, grid capacity, cooling, industrial automation, critical minerals, manufacturing resilience, cybersecurity for operational technology, and the ability to deploy technology into conservative physical industries.
Component 3

Speaker Context — The “Who”

Ricardo Angel, PhD is the Founder, CEO and Managing Partner of Piva Capital. Piva’s official biography states that he brings more than 25 years of experience investing in industrial and energy sectors. Before founding Piva in 2019, he was a founding Managing Director of GE Ventures and led investments in energy, oil and gas, industry and natural resources.

Ricardo also led Energy Technology Ventures, a $300 million joint venture formed by GE, NRG Energy and ConocoPhillips. Earlier in his career, he worked at Chevron’s venture capital group, Goldman Sachs investment banking, and the CNA Corporation. His education includes BS, MS and PhD degrees in engineering from the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and an MBA from Kellogg.

His perspective is different from a typical software VC. He understands patient capital, technical due diligence, industrial safety constraints, real assets, customer concentration, hardware scale-up, procurement cycles, supply-chain risk, and the challenge of selling innovation to conservative industrial CEOs and CFOs. For students, he is the right person to ask how AI and deep tech become investable in industries where mistakes are expensive and deployment cycles are long.

Perspective
Industrial venture capital, energy transition, technical diligence, deep-tech scaling, corporate innovation, and Asian industrial capital.

Component 4

Golden Questions — The “Ask”

These questions are designed for an executive audience. They are not factual questions that can be answered by a web search; they ask the speaker to interpret Piva’s investment thesis and connect it to Asian industrial realities.

  1. 1 Deploying AI in cloud software is relatively easy, but applying it to heavy industry involves legacy equipment, operational technology, safety constraints, and conservative procurement. What is the Silicon Valley playbook for successfully bridging deep-tech software with legacy industrial assets?
  2. 2 Given rising geopolitical tensions and supply-chain fragmentation in Asia, how does Piva assess investment risk for deep-tech startups that rely on globalized hardware manufacturing, critical minerals, batteries, grid components, or specialized industrial suppliers?
  3. 3 Piva has an LP base anchored by PETRONAS and a thesis in industrial transformation that will require Asian markets to scale. Which Asian markets are you most bullish on for industrial AI and energy-transition technologies over the next five years, and why?
  4. 4 Software VCs are paying high multiples for AI startups, while many deep-tech valuations remain disciplined because deployment is harder. Will the AI software boom eventually fund the physical infrastructure transition, like the dot-com boom helped fund internet infrastructure, or will it pull capital away from harder industrial problems?
  5. 5 In industrial AI, the customer often says “we want innovation,” but procurement, safety, unions, regulators, and asset owners move slowly. What signals tell you that a startup can actually cross the gap from pilot to scaled industrial deployment?
  6. 6 Many Chinese and Southeast Asian industrial enterprises want to fund their own AI transitions. Should they build internal corporate venture arms, partner with specialist funds like Piva, acquire startups, or become first customers for emerging technologies?
  7. 7 For AI startups serving utilities, factories, and critical infrastructure, what is more defensible: proprietary algorithms, proprietary data, hardware integration, regulatory approvals, channel access, customer trust, or switching costs?
  8. 8 AI data centers are increasing power demand and straining grids. From Piva’s perspective, where are the most underappreciated investment opportunities created by AI’s physical footprint: power generation, grid transmission, cooling, energy storage, cybersecurity, materials, or industrial automation?
Student preparation prompt

Before the Piva visit, choose one physical-industry problem in Asia — grid reliability, port logistics, factory automation, mining safety, oil and gas operations, green steel, data-center power, water infrastructure, construction productivity, or critical-infrastructure cybersecurity.

Ask: What would make this problem venture-investable: technical breakthrough, customer willingness to pay, regulatory tailwind, supply-chain advantage, defensible data, hardware integration, or a credible path from pilot to industrial scale?
