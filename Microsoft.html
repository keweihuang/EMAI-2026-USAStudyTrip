Component 1

Company Overview

Microsoft was founded in 1975 by Bill Gates and Paul Allen. It grew from PC operating systems and productivity software into one of the world’s largest enterprise technology platforms, spanning productivity, cloud infrastructure, security, developer tools, gaming, devices, business applications, and now AI copilots and agents.

Microsoft’s current AI strategy is built on three reinforcing assets. First, it has massive enterprise distribution through Microsoft 365, Teams, Outlook, Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Dynamics, Power Platform, GitHub, Windows, and LinkedIn. Second, it has cloud infrastructure through Azure and Azure AI Foundry. Third, it has model access and model development through Azure OpenAI Service, OpenAI partnership rights, Anthropic models in Microsoft Foundry, open and third-party models, the Phi small-model family, and Microsoft’s own MAI models.

For this study trip, Microsoft should be viewed not only as a software vendor, but as one of the strongest cases of enterprise AI distribution. The strategic question is whether Copilot and agents can convert daily work activity into measurable business value, and whether Microsoft can help customers move beyond pilots into changed workflows, governance, and operating models.

Component 2

Strategic Context — The “What”

Microsoft has staked its AI future on integrating generative AI across the entire enterprise technology stack. Microsoft 365 Copilot brings AI into daily knowledge work; GitHub Copilot changes software development; Copilot Studio allows organizations to build agents; Azure AI Foundry supports model selection, app development, evaluation, and deployment; and Azure OpenAI Service gives enterprises access to OpenAI models with Azure-grade controls.

The first strategic story is AI as a daily co-worker. Microsoft’s Copilot vision is not just about one chatbot. It is about embedding AI into meetings, email, documents, spreadsheets, coding, security operations, customer workflows, and business applications. The core promise is productivity and capacity: helping workers create, analyze, summarize, decide, and collaborate faster.

The second strategic story is model diversification. Microsoft remains deeply linked to OpenAI, but it is also expanding customer choice. Microsoft Foundry offers models from Azure OpenAI, Anthropic Claude, Meta, Mistral AI, DeepSeek, xAI, Cohere, Hugging Face, NVIDIA, and others. Microsoft has also developed the Phi family of small language models and announced MAI models such as MAI-Transcribe-1, MAI-Voice-1, and MAI-Image-2 in Microsoft Foundry. This reduces concentration risk and lets customers choose different models for cost, latency, accuracy, sovereignty, or task fit.

The third strategic story is industrializing change management. Microsoft’s 2026 Work Trend Index analyzed anonymized Microsoft 365 productivity signals and surveyed 20,000 AI-using workers across 10 countries. Its main message is that the bottleneck is often not individual willingness or tool capability, but whether organizations redesign work, roles, incentives, manager support, and workflows around AI. Microsoft’s own framing says workers are often ready, but organizational systems are not.

The most important executive issue is therefore ROI. Many organizations buy Copilot or AI tools, run pilots, and report time saved. But CFOs need a stronger value story: revenue impact, cycle-time reduction, customer satisfaction, error reduction, risk reduction, software delivery speed, sales productivity, employee experience, and quality of decisions. Microsoft’s Copilot product pages now emphasize measuring Copilot and agent usage trends alongside a company’s own business metrics.

Executive warning: Access to Copilot does not automatically create transformation. The hardest step is moving from “AI added to existing work” to “work redesigned around AI,” including governance, training, incentives, integration, and measurement.
Executive interpretation: Microsoft’s advantage is not only model access. It is enterprise distribution, identity, security, compliance, developer tools, data, productivity workflows, and the ability to make AI part of everyday work. Its challenge is proving that enterprise-wide AI deployments produce measurable business value beyond impressive demos.
Component 3

Speaker Context — The “Who”

Shaloo Garg is publicly described as Managing Director for Microsoft’s Late Stage Startups & Unicorns program, based in Silicon Valley. Her role places her at the intersection of Microsoft, Azure, venture-backed AI companies, late-stage startups, enterprise customers, investors, and strategic partnerships.

Her background is particularly relevant because she combines startup, enterprise, strategic partnership, and corporate development experience. Public speaker bios describe her earlier work at Oracle leading global corporate innovation, go-to-market for Oracle Cloud, and participation in M&A transactions involving PeopleSoft, Siebel, and Retek. She is also associated with Tech for Good and UN Women innovation work, including initiatives aimed at digital literacy and education access.

Students should expect a perspective different from a standard enterprise software pitch. Shaloo can discuss what AI-native startups are actually building, what it takes for unicorns to scale on Azure, how enterprise buyers evaluate startup risk, and why large companies often struggle to partner with or acquire AI-native firms. Public materials link Microsoft’s startup ecosystem to companies such as Harvey and Moveworks, while Microsoft’s Anthropic announcement includes Replit as a customer example for Claude models in Microsoft Foundry.

Her value for EMAi students is the contrast between AI-native companies and legacy enterprises. Startups often move quickly, design around AI from the start, and measure value through usage and growth. Large enterprises often carry legacy systems, slower procurement, risk committees, integration debt, and organizational resistance. That tension is central to the session.

Component 4

Golden Questions — The “Ask”

These questions are designed for an executive audience. They are not factual questions that can be answered by a web search; they ask the speaker to interpret Microsoft’s strategy and connect it to Asian enterprise realities.

  1. 1 The ROI of deploying enterprise-wide AI tools like Copilot is heavily debated. What metrics should a CFO actually use to measure true productivity gains beyond “hours saved” — for example, revenue lift, cycle-time reduction, error reduction, customer experience, software velocity, or risk reduction?
  2. 2 Microsoft has more real-world Copilot deployment data than almost any company. Stripping away the marketing, what is the single biggest predictor of whether a customer generates measurable productivity gains rather than staying in pilot mode?
  3. 3 From your vantage point working with unicorns, what is the most common operational blind spot legacy enterprises have when they try to partner with, buy from, or acquire AI-native startups?
  4. 4 Microsoft’s AI strategy still benefits from OpenAI, but Microsoft is also working with Anthropic, Mistral, Meta, DeepSeek, xAI, its Phi small models, and MAI models. From the startup-partnership lens, are AI startups consolidating around a few foundation-model providers, or fragmenting across many models for different tasks?
  5. 5 In China, many executives cannot directly rely on the same OpenAI or Anthropic stack used by US companies. How should Chinese or China-exposed enterprises think about building an AI architecture that remains compatible with global platforms while respecting local model ecosystems and data rules?
  6. 6 Microsoft’s Work Trend Index suggests that organizational factors can matter more than individual enthusiasm. In Asian enterprises, where hierarchy, consensus, and risk avoidance can be stronger, what change-management practices most improve Copilot adoption?
  7. 7 AI-native startups often build products around agents and workflows from day one. If you were advising a traditional bank, insurer, or logistics company in Singapore, what should it copy from AI-native startups, and what should it deliberately avoid copying?
  8. 8 Microsoft has the distribution to make AI tools available to everyone in an enterprise. But broad availability can also create uncontrolled usage. How should executives balance democratized AI access with governance, data protection, and compliance?
Student preparation prompt

Before the Microsoft visit, choose one knowledge-work process in your organization — management reporting, legal review, sales preparation, software development, compliance review, meeting follow-up, customer support, or internal research.

Ask: If Copilot or an AI agent improved this process, what would count as real ROI: faster work, better decisions, fewer errors, higher revenue, better customer experience, lower risk, or improved employee capability?
